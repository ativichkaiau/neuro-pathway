        Guide

        How to use the simulator

        Overview

        Trace flow. Interrupt it. Localize the sign.

        This simulator turns the classic neurology reasoning chain into something you can poke. Every session follows the same three beats:

        1. Flow — start from an intact tract so the baseline signal is obvious.
        2. Interrupt — drop a lesion somewhere along the path and watch which fibers fall silent.
        3. Localize — read the visual break against decussation, somatotopy, and the clinical pattern that drops out.

        The canvas is the source of truth. The side panels just narrate what the lines are doing.

        Step 02 · Left panel

        Pick a pathway

        The Pathway list in the left panel holds the major tracts the simulator models — corticospinal, dorsal columns, spinothalamic, optic, and friends.

        • Click any pathway to load it onto the canvas.
        • The selected tract glows; its origin and target light up.
        • The cockpit footer updates with the tract's origin, decussation, signal rule, and pattern.
        • The right panel resets to the intact baseline — useful before you place a lesion.

        Always read the intact baseline first. It tells you what the lesion is about to take away.

        Step 03 · Left panel

        Place a lesion

        The Lesion Site list lets you drop a focal lesion at any level of the active tract.

        • Choose a site — cortex, internal capsule, brainstem level, cord segment, peripheral nerve.
        • The canvas marks the lesion with a red core and an aura halo.
        • Fibers downstream of the break switch to a dashed muted stroke.
        • The right panel rewrites the clinical output to reflect the deficit.

        Click the same lesion again — or pick a different pathway — to clear it and return to baseline.

        Step 04 · Workspace

        Read the canvas

        The schematic uses a small color vocabulary:

        • Active flow — fibers carrying signal intact.
        • Lesion — the interruption point.
        • Downstream affected — fibers past the break, now silent.

        The dashed midline runs vertically. Anything crossing it is decussating. The brain, brainstem, cord, eyes, and ears are anatomical anchors — match the tract's course against them when you reason about laterality.

        Step 05 · Right panel

        Interpret the clinical output

        The right panel narrates the canvas in clinical language.

        • Clinical output — the headline finding (e.g. "Left UMN weakness").
        • Deficit summary — motor, sensory, reflexes, laterality, network, and the classic pitfall to avoid.
        • Why this happens — the reasoning chain from anatomy to sign.
        • Localization clue — the cardinal feature that nails the level.

        If a deficit feels wrong, re-read the canvas. The text follows the lines, not the other way round.

        Step 06 · Left panel

        Use the classification systems

        The Classification tabs at the top of the left panel let you label the lesion against a classical anatomical system — Brodmann areas, brainstem syndromes, spinal levels, subcortical territories, and more.

        • Switch tabs to change the lens.
        • Pick an entry to apply that classification to the current canvas state.
        • The right panel shows the classification card with region, signature, localization, pitfall, and reasoning steps.

        This is the layer that mirrors how textbooks and boards talk about lesions.

        Step 07 · Below the canvas

        Use the cross-section viewer

        The Cross Section panel sits below the cockpit. It slices the brainstem or spinal cord at clinically useful levels and shows where the active tract sits inside that slice.

        • Switch the tab to change levels (e.g. brainstem vs. cord).
        • The highlighted region maps the tract's position at that level.
        • The localization rule restates the principle in one sentence.
        • Structures listed below are the neighbors most often co-affected.
